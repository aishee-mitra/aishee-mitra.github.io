title: "The Archaeology of Old Versions"
date: 2026-08-28
excerpt: "There is a specific, humbling kind of horror in reading things you wrote six months ago. It’s a reminder that the 'current me' is only the latest version of a very confused series of drafts."

I recently stumbled upon a notebook entry from early last year, and for a moment, I genuinely didn't recognize the person who wrote it. Not because the handwriting had changed, but because the logic was so... *earnest* yet completely wrong. I had spent three pages meticulously arguing for a solution that I now know is fundamentally flawed. 

The immediate, visceral instinct was to rip the page out. There is a powerful urge to curate our own histories, to scrub away the evidence of our previous misconceptions so that we can present a seamless narrative of competence. We do this with our digital lives constantly—deleting the "test" commits, rewriting the first draft of a proposal, or archiving the social media posts that no longer fit our current "brand." We want the world to see the final, polished artifact, not the messy scaffolding that held it up.

But I've started to realize that there is a strange, quiet value in this kind of personal archaeology. 

When I look at that old, flawed notebook entry, I'm not just seeing a mistake; I'm seeing the exact boundary of what I understood at the time. If I delete the evidence of the struggle, I lose the scale of the progress. The "cringe" we feel when revisiting old work is actually a very reliable metric of growth. If you look back at something you did a year ago and think, *Wow, I really nailed that,* you might actually be in trouble. It means your delta is zero. 

The real win isn't in being right all the time; it's in the speed and frequency with which you can look at your past self and say, "I know better now."

I've decided to stop the aggressive pruning. I'm keeping the messy drafts and the "wrong" versions of my thoughts. Not because they are useful as references—they aren't—but because they serve as a reminder that being "wrong" is the only prerequisite for eventually being right. 

It turns out that the most honest version of a person isn't the polished final draft. It's the entire, chaotic history of the edits.
