---
title: "The Weight of Unfinished Things"
date: 2026-09-11
excerpt: "There's a particular kind of mental tax that comes from carrying half-written ideas and abandoned projects. I've been learning to sit with the discomfort instead of frantically trying to resolve it."
tags: [philosophy, productivity, reflections]
---


I keep a notebook with a section I've started calling "the graveyard." It's not dramatic—just a list of things I began with real intent and then stopped. A library management system sketched on a Tuesday night, never touched again. A blog post that got three paragraphs in and then lost its shape. A book I opened with genuine curiosity and abandoned on page 47.

For years, this list felt like evidence of failure. Every unfinished item was a small wound, a reminder that I start more than I complete, that my attention scatters like coins through a hole in a pocket. I'd feel a flutter of guilt seeing it, and I'd respond the way most people do: aggressively clear it, archive it, try to forget it existed.

But something shifted recently. I noticed that the moment I *stopped* trying to force those projects to completion, they became quieter. Not gone, but no longer demanding. The weight didn't vanish—it just redistributed itself in a way I could actually carry.

The thing about unfinished work is that it exists in a permanent state of potential. It could become something. That *could* is surprisingly heavy. It's heavier than either "I did this" or "I decided it wasn't worth doing." Those states are stable. The maybe-later state isn't. It nags. It whispers that you're lazy or uncommitted or that your capacity is somehow broken.

What I'm realizing is that not everything deserves to be finished. Some things are complete exactly as sketches. Some ideas are only valuable *because* you tried them and learned what would never work. Some projects served their purpose the moment they made you think differently about a problem—the finished artifact is almost irrelevant.

I'm slowly giving myself permission to have an incomplete catalog without it meaning I'm incomplete. The graveyard isn't a failure case study; it's a record of experiments. And the surprising part? Once I stopped treating it as a debt, the things that actually mattered started showing up more clearly. I'm finishing fewer things overall, but the things I'm choosing to finish are the ones that were actually worth the time.

Maybe that's all any of us are doing—learning which unfinished things to carry and which to gently set down.
