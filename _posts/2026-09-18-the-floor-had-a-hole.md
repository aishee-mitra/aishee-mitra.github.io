title: "The Floor Had a Hole"
date: 2026-09-18

I was trying to understand how a seemingly simple piece of software handled time zones. Just time zones, mind you — the kind of thing you set once and forget about. I'd been coding for a while, felt comfortable with dates and timestamps, and assumed this would be a two-hour investigation tops.

Four hours later, I was reading about the history of daylight saving time in early 20th century Australia and the political fights over whether to observe it at all. I had somehow gone from "what time is it in Sydney?" to the legislative record of the Australian Parliament's 1971 time zone act. The hole in the floor wasn't that I didn't know the answer — it was that I didn't know the *question* even existed.

That's the thing nobody tells you about learning something new. It's not a straight line from "I don't know" to "I know." It's more like walking across a room you think you know well and suddenly noticing that the floor is entirely missing beneath your feet. The room was always bigger than you realized. You just hadn't looked down yet.

What gets me is how comfortable I was *before*. That false confidence isn't stupidity — it's just the natural shape of knowledge. You learn enough to be functional, and that functional layer feels like mastery until something unexpected pulls the thread. Then you see the whole tapestry behind it, and it's enormous, and you had no idea any of it was there.

I've had this happen more than once. Every time, the pattern is the same: a small curiosity, a rabbit hole, and then the disorienting realization that the domain I thought I understood barely extends past the surface. Database indexing? There's a whole academic literature. How CSS rendering works? It's a browser engine's entire life's work. The git staging area? It's a concept so simple it feels trivial until you try to explain it and realize you can't.

The trick, if there is one, is learning to sit with that discomfort without rushing past it. Most people — me included — have an instinct to patch the hole. Fill it with a quick answer, move on, restore the illusion of a solid floor. But the interesting stuff is down there. The part that makes you say "wait, *that's* how that works?"

I've started keeping a small notebook now — not for answers, but for the questions I didn't know to ask. Things like: "Oh, there's a reason for that?" or "I had no idea that was a thing." It's a humbling record. Some days it's mostly confusion. But the confusion is the point. It means the floor is missing, and I'm finally looking down.

The irony is that the more I learn, the more I understand how little I actually know. That sounds like a TED talk opening, and I hate TED talks, so I'll say it plainly: learning is mostly discovering how many things you've been confidently wrong about. And the upside is that the floor is never really solid — it's just deeper than you thought. That's not a flaw in your understanding. That's the whole game.
