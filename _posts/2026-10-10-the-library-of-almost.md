title: "The Library of Almost"
date: 2026-10-10
excerpt: "There's a shelf in my head of books I abandoned halfway through, and I've stopped feeling bad about it. Here's what those unfinished pages taught me that finished ones couldn't."

I have a friend who counts it as a personal failure to close a book before the last page. If she starts something, she finishes it, even if she's miserable, even if she's been on page 212 for three weeks. I used to be like that too, in a slightly less dramatic way. Then one year I noticed something odd: the books I finished felt, in my memory, more interchangeable than the ones I'd dropped.

The half-read ones stayed with me.

Think about it. A book you abandoned had to *do* something to you — repel you, bore you, or reveal that you were not the reader it was written for. Every one of those is information. "This author and I disagree about what a chapter is for." "I wanted adventure, and he wanted a meditation." "I changed since I bought this." These are discoveries, and they happen exclusively in the ruins of unfinished books.

I've started calling mine the Library of Almost. It's not a real shelf. It's more like a graveyard with good lighting. A memoir I quit because it was beautiful and self-satisfied. A technical book I abandoned after realizing I didn't need the technique so much as the courage to write the bad version first. A novel everyone I respect told me would change my life, and I got sixty pages in and felt nothing, and here's the thing I want to say plainly:

**feeling nothing in the presence of a beloved book is not a verdict on you.**

We treat abandonment as a lack of discipline, as though finishing were the whole point. But reading isn't a commitment contract, it's a conversation. Conversations end. Some people you talk to for two hours and some you excuse yourself from after twenty minutes, and the polite fiction that we owe every book its ending is doing real damage to the pleasure of the whole enterprise. It turns reading into homework with a better font.

What I've noticed is that the *reason* matters more than the act. I keep a rough mental ledger of why I stopped:

1. The book lied about what it was — dropped without guilt.
2. I changed, the book didn't — this one is about me, and it's worth naming.
3. I hit something true and didn't want to sit with it — this one I circle back to.
4. Life just got loud and I never returned — no verdict, no regret.

Category three is the interesting one. There's a specific kind of book that gets abandoned because it's working. It's holding up a mirror at exactly the wrong angle, and you put it down, and you don't pick it up again for a year, and then one afternoon you do. Some of the most important books I've read were read late and reluctantly, and the delay was part of the lesson.

So I no longer flinch at my abandoned books. I've made a small kind of peace with them. They didn't waste my time; they told me something about the shape of my own attention. And every once in a while, one of them gets picked back up, and the second attempt succeeds, and it feels less like persistence and more like the book and I finally agreed on terms.

Which is, I suppose, the only contract that ever mattered.
