---
title: "The Patience of Worn-Out Things"
date: 2026-10-17
excerpt: "Some of the things I love most are the ones that have officially stopped earning their keep. Here is why I've stopped apologizing for keeping them."
tags: [philosophy, daily life, objects, attachment, minimalism, self-reflection]

I own a pair of earphones that don't work anymore. One side died months ago, and I kept using the other side like some kind of audio pirate until that one went too. I meant to throw them out. That was four months ago. They're still on my desk, coiled neatly next to my working pair like a retired couple watching the new tenants move in.

This is not a post about minimalism. I'm not going to tell you to declutter your life. I'm going to make the opposite case — that some of the things worth keeping are the ones that have officially stopped earning their keep.

There's a particular joy in an object that has outlived its usefulness. A t-shirt so thin it's basically a rumor of a t-shirt. A notebook where the first half is full of plans and the second half is empty because the plans changed. A pen that's down to its last drop of ink, writing in a faint, desperate hand. These things have stopped being tools and started being something else — witnesses, maybe. Or souvenirs of a version of you that needed them.

**Usefulness is a terrible measure of what deserves to stay.** We talk about objects like they're employees — they perform, they get compensated (by being kept), and when they stop performing, they're let go. But that's a cold way to relate to the physical world around you. Some of the things I love most are the ones that could never justify their existence by any practical metric.

My grandmother kept a steel container that once held expensive biscuits. When the biscuits were gone, it became a container for rubber bands. When the rubber bands were lost, it held buttons. When the buttons were used, it sat empty on her shelf for years. It was never useful again after the biscuits, but it was never in the way either. It was just... there. A small, round, steel fact.

I think about that container when I'm cleaning my desk and reach for something broken. The instinct is to tidy, to sort into keep-and-discard. But sometimes the discard pile feels like a small funeral, and not every funeral needs to happen on schedule.

**Some things earn the right to be useless.** A mug with a chip in it, held together by habit and a faint tea stain that won't wash out. A bookmark that's been moved through six books and will move through six more. A key to a lock that no longer exists on any door I own. These aren't failures. They're retirees. They've done their time and now they get to just exist on the shelf, reminding me that value doesn't have to be renewable.

I'm not saying hoard everything. I'm saying that when something breaks, there's a version of grief in letting it go — and that grief deserves a moment of respect before you toss it in the bin.

My dead earphones are still on my desk. I don't know when I'll throw them away. Maybe never. Maybe that's the point.
