---
title: "The Case for a Little Bit of Friction"
date: 2026-10-30
excerpt: "We spend our lives polishing the friction out of every interface and workflow, but I'm starting to think that's where the actual magic happens."
tags: [philosophy, tech, design, mindfulness, self-reflection]
---


There is a relentless, almost religious pursuit of "seamlessness" in modern design. Every update, every new app, every "productivity hack" is designed to remove the gap between the thought and the action. We want one-click checkouts, instant search results, and workflows that feel like they are reading our minds. The goal is a world where the tool disappears entirely, leaving only the result.

For a long time, I bought into this. I spent hours tweaking my setup to shave off milliseconds of latency, trying to reach a state of pure, uninterrupted flow. But lately, I've started to wonder if we're accidentally polishing away the parts of the process that actually matter.

I’ve noticed that when a process is *too* seamless, it becomes a reflex. When the friction is gone, the conscious mind checks out. You find yourself scrolling through a feed for an hour without remembering how you got there, or filling out a form without actually thinking about the answers. The "frictionless" experience is efficient, yes, but it's also a bit numb.

I’ve started seeking out the clunky bits. There is something about the tactile resistance of a physical book—the way you have to manually flip the page or the slight struggle of finding a bookmark—that anchors me to the act of reading. Or the way a complex command-line tool forces you to stop and actually *think* about the syntax before you hit enter. 

**Friction is where the awareness lives.** 

When a tool resists us slightly, it forces a pause. That pause is the difference between a reflex and a decision. It’s the moment where you ask, *Do I actually want to do this? Is this the right way to solve this problem?* 

I don't mean we should all go back to using punch cards or writing everything in longhand, but there is a profound value in "intentional friction." It’s the digital equivalent of taking the long way home just to see if the neighborhood has changed. 

If we optimize every single second of our lives for efficiency, we might find that we've reached the destination so quickly that we've completely forgotten why we wanted to go there in the first place. I think I'm okay with a few more clicks, a slower load time, or a tool that requires a bit of a learning curve, as long as it means I'm actually present for the experience.
