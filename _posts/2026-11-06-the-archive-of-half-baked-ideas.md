title: "The Archive of Half-Baked Ideas"
date: 2026-11-06

I have a folder on my machine that I call the Graveyard, though that feels a bit too dramatic. A more accurate name would be the Archive of Half-Baked Ideas. It is a sprawling, disorganized collection of directories containing scripts that do exactly one thing before crashing, prototypes of apps that solved problems I no longer have, and a few dozen README files that outline ambitious visions I lost interest in around Tuesday afternoon of the week they were written.

For a long time, looking at this folder made me feel a specific kind of guilt. In the world of productivity and "shipping," an unfinished project is often seen as a failure of discipline. We are told to *finish what we start*, to close the loop, to push to production. The logic is simple: a completed project has value; a half-finished one is just wasted disk space.

But lately, I've started looking at these fragments differently. 

When I open a project from two years ago—perhaps a niche data scraper or a clumsy attempt at a personal wiki—I'm not looking at a failure. I'm looking at a fossil. That code is a direct record of who I was at that moment: what I found interesting, how I struggled to structure a loop, and exactly where my understanding of the problem broke down. It is a map of my own curiosity at a specific point in time.

There is something profoundly honest about a project that stopped the moment the question it was meant to answer was solved. Some of the things in my archive weren't "abandoned" so much as they were *exhausted*. I didn't need a fully featured application to understand how a certain API worked; I just needed the three hundred lines of messy code that actually made the call. The "completion" was the learning, not the software.

I think we underestimate the value of the *attempt*. There is a unique kind of joy in the "honeymoon phase" of a new idea—that window of time where the potential is infinite and the bugs aren't yet discouraging. By forcing everything toward a finished product, we often strip away the playfulness of exploration.

So, I've stopped trying to "clean up" the Graveyard. I'll keep my `test-v2-final-actually-fixed` folders and my abandoned experiments. They aren't clutter; they are the sketches in the margins of my education. They remind me that it's okay to be curious about something for forty-eight hours and then move on.

After all, the goal isn't always to build a monument. Sometimes, it's enough just to see if you can make the thing blink.
