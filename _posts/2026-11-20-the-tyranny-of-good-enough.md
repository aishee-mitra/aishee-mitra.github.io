---
title: "The Tyranny of \"Good Enough\""
date: 2026-11-20
excerpt: "We're told that shipping is the only thing that matters, but there's a quiet, stubborn joy in spending an extra hour making a piece of code feel just... right."
tags: [tech, craft, philosophy, software, self-reflection]
---


I have a habit that my professional side finds completely irrational: I cannot leave a piece of code alone if it feels "clunky," even if it passes every single test and performs exactly as requested.

There is a specific kind of tension that happens when a solution is functionally perfect but aesthetically offensive. You know the feeling. The logic is sound, the edge cases are handled, and the output is correct. By every objective metric, you are done. You should close the laptop, go get a tea, and move on with your life. 

But then you look at that one nested loop, or that slightly-too-long variable name, and you feel a physical itch. It’s the sense that the code is *lying* about its own intent. It works, but it doesn't *express* the solution.

In the world of "move fast and break things," this impulse is often labeled as over-engineering. We are taught that "good enough" is the gold standard of productivity. After all, the user doesn't see the indentation or the way the data flows between functions; they only see the button click and the result. From a purely economic standpoint, spending an extra hour refining a function that already works is a waste of resources.

And yet, I've found that this "waste" is actually where the most profound learning happens. 

When I push myself to move from "it works" to "it's elegant," I'm not just polishing pixels. I'm forcing myself to think deeper about the problem. I'm asking: *Why is this clunky? Is it because the tool is limiting me, or because my mental model of the problem is slightly off?*

The pursuit of elegance is often a pursuit of clarity. When code is elegant, it becomes a form of communication. It tells the next person (who is usually just a future, more tired version of me) exactly what the intent was without them having to reverse-engineer the logic in their head.

There is a quiet, immense satisfaction in that moment when the logic finally "clicks"—when the complexity collapses into something simple and intuitive. It’s not about perfectionism, really. It's about the craft. It's the difference between building a house that stands up and building one where the doors actually swing open the way they were meant to.

I suspect that a lot of us carry this tension every day: the battle between the clock and the craft. I've decided that, as long as the deadline isn't screaming, I'm going to choose the craft. My productivity might take a hit, but my soul feels a lot better when the code is honest.
